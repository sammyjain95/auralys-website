From answering questions to finishing jobs: agentic AI for small businesses, through Ember

By Sumant Jain, founder of Auralys AI

The bottleneck is not answers, it is unfinished work

Most small businesses do not struggle to get an answer from AI. They struggle with the work that comes after the answer: the competitor check that never gets written up, the price comparison that stays in three open tabs, the lead list that never becomes a call list, the spreadsheet that nobody has time to turn into a decision.

Chatbots answer. Voice agents answer calls. Both are useful. Neither finishes the job.

That gap is visible in 2026 small business surveys. Thryv found 66% of United States small businesses use AI, up from 55% a year earlier, while 70% said they need more training to use it effectively.[3] Adoption is no longer the problem. Turning adoption into finished work is.

Agentic AI is the next step, and it is the step that matters most for a small business with a small team. Instead of giving you one reply, an agentic system takes a whole job, works through its steps, checks its sources, and hands back a finished deliverable you can review and use. That goal-directed, multi-step definition is how McKinsey describes agents in its 2025 State of AI report.[1]

What "agentic" means in plain language

Forget the jargon for a moment. An agentic job has four parts: a clear job stated in business language, a method so the work follows the same steps every time instead of improvising, sources so claims can be checked instead of taken on trust, and a deliverable, a report or a table, not a chat transcript you have to tidy up yourself.

That is the difference between asking AI a question and handing AI a job. The first saves a minute. The second gives you back an afternoon.

Voice AI fits inside this picture, but it is not the whole picture. Voice handles the conversation. Agentic AI handles the work behind the conversation: research, comparison, analysis, and preparation, so the person who takes the call, makes the decision, or visits the customer is already prepared.

Ember, built for the owner, not the coder

Ember is Auralys AI's desktop application for this kind of work. It is designed for a business owner who does not code, does not want to learn prompt engineering, and does not want to see a terminal, a diff, or a settings screen full of technical terms.

The idea is simple: jobs, not tools. You pick a job, answer a short form in plain questions, and Ember does the work and returns a deliverable.

Market research and reporting, first

For most small businesses, the clearest use of agentic AI is market research that actually ends in a report you can act on. This is where Ember is designed to shine, and it is the capability to judge first.

Market research in Ember is not a chat answer with a few links pasted at the end. It is a constrained job: fixed sources, fixed steps, and a fixed output shape, so the result is comparable from one run to the next.

Competitor research starts with a name or handle, a platform, and a period. Ember works from public posts and profile data only, uses fixed sample sizes, and normalises engagement per follower where the data allows. The deliverable is a one-page style brief: posting cadence, top content themes, what performs, and the gaps your business can exploit, with source links inline.

Financial research starts with a company or ticker list and a brief or deep choice. Filings and reputable financial sources come first, news second, and every figure is sourced and dated. The deliverable is a report with a figures table, and it uses no investment advice language. Unknowns are marked unknown.

Reporting is the third leg, and it is what turns research into something shareable. The report builder works only from files you provide, plus your business profile. It does not fill gaps by invention. The deliverable is a formatted report, summary first, sources at the end, exportable as a PDF. That is the difference between research you did and research you can hand to a partner, a lender, a supplier, or your team.

Together, these three jobs cover the question every owner asks before spending money: what is happening in my market, what do the numbers actually say, and can I put that in front of someone else without rewriting it?

The reporting burden behind that question is large. In Emplifi's 2027 social marketing report, 62% of marketers said they spend four or more hours a week on reporting and analytics, while 69% said AI had saved their team three or more hours a week on it.[5]

Ember is designed around six recurring jobs in total. The market research and reporting jobs above come first, followed by:

  • Website scraping and price comparison. A comparison table from public pages, one row per source, with the capture date and a source link for each row.
  • Data analysis. Upload a spreadsheet or CSV, ask the question in plain words, and get findings with charts and the numbers table beneath them, showing which columns were used and how the figures were worked out.
  • Lead finding. A leads table built from a description of your ideal customer, with businesses verifiable from their own public web presence and contact details only where the business itself has published them.

Each job ends in the same place: a deliverable you can read, share, and export as a PDF or CSV. The deliverable is the product, not the conversation that produced it.

What makes this different from another AI tool

Four design choices matter, and they are all about trust.

First, forms, not prompts. Every job starts from three to five short questions, with sensible defaults already filled in from your business profile. You tell Ember once what you sell, who your customers are, and who your competitors are, and you do not retype that context on every job. If the form does not cover what you want, you can still describe it in your own words.

Second, plain language throughout. Projects instead of sessions, assistant instead of agent, and a plain-language explanation before anything runs: what Ember will check, what it will produce, and what it will cost. The cost estimate is shown before you run, and spending is tracked, so there are no surprise bills.

Third, sources inline. Research deliverables carry source links for their claims. Finance figures are sourced and dated. Scraped rows carry their source and capture date. If something cannot be verified, it is marked unknown, not smoothed over.

Fourth, read-only research, with the owner still deciding. In this first version, Ember researches and produces documents. It does not send, post, purchase, or book anything on your behalf. Outreach, posting, and buying stay separate, approval-gated steps for a human to take. That boundary is deliberate: the machine does the legwork, the owner makes the decision.

An illustrative day with Ember

Imagine a Monday meeting. Before it, the owner wants a market picture they can actually present: what the two nearest competitors posted last month, what the numbers say about a supplier or rival, and all of it pulled together into one formatted report.

In the old way, that is three separate research tasks, each likely to be started and not finished.

With an agentic approach, each becomes a job with a form, a method, and a deliverable: a competitor brief, a sourced finance brief, and a single report, summary first and sources at the end. The owner reviews the report, corrects anything that looks wrong, and walks into the meeting with something shareable, not a set of chat answers. Nothing here is a client case study. It is an illustration of how the jobs are meant to fit together.

The honest limits

Agentic AI does not remove judgment. It moves judgment to a better place: reviewing a finished deliverable instead of doing every step by hand.

Three limits are worth stating plainly. First, a recipe is only as good as its method. An unconstrained instruction to "go research this" produces variable output, which is why each Ember job fixes its sources, steps, and output shape in advance. Second, this first version works from public data and files you provide. It does not yet connect to your social, accounting, or CRM accounts, so jobs that need private data will still need a human to supply the file or the link. That restraint matters: Pax8 found 35% of AI-engaged small businesses had discovered unapproved tools, workflows, or agents running in their operations in the previous six months.[4] Gartner has also forecast, as a forecast rather than an observed rate, that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027 because of escalating costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls.[2] Third, a desktop workbench is a new habit. For some owners, the first buyer will be the consultant or agency running Ember on their behalf, with client-presentable deliverables as the output. That is a legitimate path, not a failure of the idea.

A tool that finishes a job badly is worse than a tool that never starts it. That is why the deliverable, the sources, and the human review matter more than the speed of the first draft.

Where to start

Do not start with six jobs. Start with one recurring market research or reporting job that currently eats an afternoon and produces nothing you can share, for example a monthly competitor check or a report you rebuild from files every time.

Pick the job, run it once, and judge Ember on the deliverable: is it readable, is it sourced, and would you send it to a colleague or a customer without rewriting it? If the answer is yes, that job is now a system instead of a chore. If the answer is no, the method needs fixing, and that is a product conversation worth having, not a reason to go back to doing it all by hand.

Agentic AI will not replace the owner. It will replace the unfinished work that currently fills the owner's week. For a small business, that is the more valuable promise, and it is the one Ember is built to keep.
